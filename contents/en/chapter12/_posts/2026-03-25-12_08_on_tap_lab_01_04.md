A 45-minute timed review exam isn’t about memorizing obscure formulas—it tests whether you can move **fluidly and calmly** across the four core pillars of Labs 1–4: discrete Bayes tables, simulation verification, continuous grid normalization, and exact Beta–Binomial / Gamma–Poisson conjugate updates!

This review guide gives you a rapid pre-exam calibration map and a 3-minute **complete end-to-end warm-up problem** so you walk into the exam with sharp numerical instincts.

## Background Knowledge Map: The Four-Lab Synthesis Checklist (Labs 1–4)

1. **Lab 1 (Discrete Bayes):** Joint tables, base-rate traps, and verifying $$P(H\mid E) = \frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E)}$$ sums to 1.
2. **Lab 2 (Simulation & Sequential Updating):** Filtering simulated worlds to approximate posteriors and confirming sequential updates match batch updates.
3. **Lab 3 (Continuous Grid):** Remembering `w = unnorm / unnorm.sum()` vs. `pdf = w / dtheta`, and using log-space for stability.
4. **Lab 4 (Conjugacy):** Beta–Binomial $$\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+k, \beta+n-k)$$ and Gamma–Poisson $$\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha+\sum k_i, \beta+n)$$ with `scipy`’s `scale = 1 / rate`!

## Review exam

Total: **10 points**

### Problem 1. Conditional probability and Bayes (2 points)

There are three bags of marbles:

- Bag 1 has `7` red marbles and `3` blue marbles.
- Bag 2 has `5` red marbles and `5` blue marbles.
- Bag 3 has `2` red marbles and `8` blue marbles.

Pick a bag at random, then draw one marble at random from that bag.

1. Compute the probability that the drawn marble is red.
2. Given that the drawn marble is red, compute the probability that it came from **Bag 1**.

Requirement: show the formulas and the final result.

### Problem 2. Monte Carlo simulation for the Monty Hall problem (2.5 points)

In the Monty Hall game there are `3` doors; only `1` hides a car and the other `2` hide goats. The player picks a door at random. The host always opens one of the remaining doors that hides a goat, then lets the player **stay** or **switch**.

1. Write a program that simulates `10000` plays.
2. Estimate the probability of winning if:
   - the player always **stays** with the initial choice;
   - the player always **switches**.
3. Conclude which strategy to choose.

Requirement: submit a short code snippet and state the two empirical probabilities you obtained.

### Problem 3. The normal distribution and computing probabilities (2.5 points)

Suppose the heights of students in a class follow a normal distribution:

$$
X \sim N(165, 8^2).
$$

1. Compute the probability that a randomly chosen student is taller than `170 cm`.
2. Compute the probability that the height is between `160 cm` and `175 cm`.
3. Find the value $$x$$ such that:

$$
P(X \le x) = 0.9.
$$

Requirement: you may use `scipy.stats` for the computation, but state each requested value clearly.

### Problem 4. Bayesian updating with the Beta-Binomial model (3 points)

Let $$p$$ be the probability that a student answers a multiple-choice question correctly. Before observing data, assume:

$$
p \sim \text{Beta}(2,2).
$$

We observe the results of `10` independent questions; the student answers `7` correctly.

1. Find the posterior distribution of $$p$$.
2. Compute the posterior mean of $$p$$.
3. Compute the posterior probability:

$$
P(p > 0.6 \mid \text{data}).
$$

4. Plot the prior and posterior densities on the same axes.

Requirement: state the update formula clearly and use Python to help with the computation or the plot.

### Worked Numerical Example: 3-Minute Full-Cycle Warm-Up (Labs 1–4 Combined)

Test yourself on one compact problem touching every Lab 1–4 skill:
- **Setup:** Prior $$\theta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2, 3)$$ (mean $$2/5 = 0.40$$). Observe $$k = 4$$ successes in $$n = 5$$ trials.
- **Conjugate Posterior (Lab 4):** $$\mathrm{Beta}(2 + 4,\; 3 + 1) = \mathbf{\mathrm{Beta}(6, 4)}$$.
- **Posterior Mean & Mode:** Mean $$= \frac{6}{10} = \mathbf{0.60}$$; Mode $$= \frac{6-1}{10-2} = \frac{5}{8} = \mathbf{0.625}$$.
- **Posterior Predictive (Next Trial):** $$P(y_{\text{new}} = 1 \mid D) = \mathbb{E}[\theta\mid D] = \mathbf{0.60}$$!
- **Grid Sanity Check (Lab 3):** On a 1,001-point grid (`dtheta = 0.001`), `(theta * w).sum()` must equal `0.6000` within `1e-4`!

### Deep-Dive Explanation: Four Exam-Day Sanity Checks That Catch Silly Mistakes (Read Slowly)

1. **Check bounds immediately:** Every probability and Beta mean must lie strictly in $$[0, 1]$$; every Gamma rate must be $$> 0$$.
2. **Check the "pull" direction:** Your posterior mean *must* sit strictly between your prior mean (`0.40`) and your raw sample rate (`4/5 = 0.80`)—if you get `0.85` or `0.30`, you swapped $$\alpha$$ and $$\beta$$!
3. **Check `scale = 1/rate` on any Gamma question:** Verify `dist.mean() == alpha / beta` right in Python before answering.
4. **Budget your 45 minutes cleanly:** Spend 10 minutes per lab section and keep 5 minutes at the end to verify sums, shapes, and interpretations in plain English.

**Quick check.** In the warm-up problem above (prior mean `0.40`, observed sample rate `0.80`), if your code printed a posterior mean of `0.35`, how would you know *in 2 seconds* that there is a bug?
