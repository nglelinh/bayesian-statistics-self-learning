In Lesson 2.6, we watched grid approximation hit an impenetrable exponential wall: even a 10-parameter model requires $$10^{20}$$ grid evaluations. Yet every day, modern statisticians fit Bayesian models with *thousands* of parameters in seconds. How do they compute a thousand-dimensional integral without ever laying down a grid? They stop trying to map the entire desert and instead send a clever random walker—a **Markov chain**—engineered so that the fraction of time it spends in every region equals the exact posterior probability of that region.

**Lesson Roadmap:** From intractable integrals to Markov chain samplers → Background Knowledge Map (Detailed balance & stationary distributions, Metropolis–Hastings & Gibbs, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo & convergence diagnostics) → Hand-worked 2-step Metropolis–Hastings & ESS calculation → Full algorithmic theory, code, and exercises.

## Learning objectives

After this lesson, you need to firmly grasp **why MCMC works** from a mathematical point of view. Specifically, you need to understand: (1) the Monte Carlo principle - sampling to estimate integrals, (2) the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm - the accept/reject mechanism and the proof of convergence, (3) Gibbs sampling - sampling each parameter conditionally, and (4) the convergence diagnostics R-hat and ESS.

> **Quick check.** Why don't we simply draw random samples directly from the posterior, instead of using a Markov chain to gradually "probe" into the high-probability region?

## Background Knowledge Map: The Mathematical Engine Behind MCMC

### 1. Stationary Distributions and Detailed Balance
- **What it is:** Designing a Markov transition kernel $$T(\theta \to \theta')$$ that satisfies **detailed balance** $$\pi(\theta) T(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta') T(\theta' \to \theta)$$ for the target posterior $$\pi(\theta) \propto p(D \mid \theta)p(\theta)$$.
- **What it studies:** Guaranteeing that the chain converges to $$\pi$$ *without ever needing the normalizing constant $$P(D)$$*, because $$P(D)$$ cancels out in every probability ratio $$\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)$$.

### 2. Proposal Geometries: Random-Walk MH, Gibbs, and HMC
- **What it is:** Three escalating strategies for proposing $$\theta^*$$: blind local steps (Metropolis–Hastings), exact conditional updates (Gibbs), and gradient-guided Hamiltonian trajectories (HMC/NUTS).
- **What it studies:** Why random walks diffuse slowly ($$O(d^2)$$ steps) in high dimensions whereas HMC surfs the **typical set** along level curves of energy using $$\nabla \log \pi(\theta)$$.

### 3. Convergence Diagnostics: $$\hat{R}$$ and Effective Sample Size (ESS)
- **What it is:** Measuring whether multiple independent chains mix into the same distribution ($$\hat{R} \approx 1.00$$) and how many *effectively independent* draws remain after accounting for autocorrelation $$\rho_k$$.
- **What it studies:** Protecting against silent sampler failure where 4,000 correlated draws actually contain less information than 50 independent ones.

## Deep-Dive Explanation: Two Metropolis–Hastings Steps & ESS by Hand (Read Slowly)

### Part 1: Two Exact MH Steps Without the Normalizing Constant
Target the standard normal $$\pi(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\theta^2/2}$$ using only the unnormalized kernel $$\tilde{\pi}(\theta) = e^{-\theta^2/2}$$ and a symmetric proposal (so $$q(\theta \mid \theta^*)/q(\theta^* \mid \theta) = 1$$). Start at $$\theta_0 = 2.0$$:

1. **Step 1 (propose $$\theta^* = 1.0$$):**
   $$ r = \frac{\tilde{\pi}(1.0)}{\tilde{\pi}(2.0)} = \frac{e^{-1^2/2}}{e^{-2^2/2}} = e^{-0.5 + 2.0} = e^{1.5} \approx 4.482. $$
   Acceptance probability $$A = \min(1, 4.482) = \mathbf{1.0}$$ → **Accept** uphill move: $$\theta_1 = 1.0$$.
2. **Step 2 (from $$\theta_1 = 1.0$$, propose $$\theta^* = 2.5$$):**
   $$ r = \frac{\tilde{\pi}(2.5)}{\tilde{\pi}(1.0)} = \frac{e^{-6.25/2}}{e^{-1/2}} = e^{-2.625} \approx \mathbf{0.0724}. $$
   Draw uniform $$u = 0.25$$. Since $$u = 0.25 > 0.0724$$ → **Reject** and record the current state again: $$\theta_2 = 1.0$$. Notice we never used $$\sqrt{2\pi}$$ once!

### Part 2: Why Autocorrelation Shrinks Your Sample Size (ESS)
Suppose a chain produces $$N = 1{,}000$$ draws with AR(1) lag-1 autocorrelation $$\rho_1 = 0.8$$ (so $$\rho_k = 0.8^k$$). The integrated autocorrelation time is:
$$ \tau_{\text{int}} = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_1^k = \frac{1 + \rho_1}{1 - \rho_1} = \frac{1.8}{0.2} = \mathbf{9.0} \implies \mathrm{ESS} = \frac{1{,}000}{9.0} \approx \mathbf{111}. $$
One thousand sluggish draws deliver only **111** independent samples—which is why ESS, not raw draw count, governs Monte Carlo precision!

## 1. From a real problem to an "incomputable" integral

### 1.1. What does Bayesian inference give you, as you already know?

When we have data $$D$$ and want to infer the parameter $$\theta$$, Bayes gives us:

$$p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta) \cdot p(\theta)}{p(D)}.$$

But **that is only a formula**. To really understand the data, we need to answer concrete questions:

**Question 1 - What is the mean?**
If we want to know the expected value of $$\theta$$ after observing the data, we need to compute:

$$\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] = \int \theta \cdot p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta.$$

**Question 2 - Which range of values contains 95% of the probability mass?**
We need to find two thresholds $$a$$ and $$b$$ satisfying:

$$\int_a^b p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta = 0.95.$$

**Question 3 - What is the probability that $$\theta$$ exceeds a threshold $$c$$?**
For example, the probability that $$\theta > 0$$ (positive slope):

$$p(\theta > c \mid D) = \int_c^\infty p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta.$$

> **Remark:** All of these natural questions lead to **integrals**!

### 1.2. The problem: the "matrix" hidden in the Bayes formula

Look closely again at the Bayes formula:

$$p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta) \cdot p(\theta)}{p(D)}.$$

We already know the **numerator** - it is:
- $$p(D \mid \theta)$$: the likelihood (easy to compute, given by the model)
- $$p(\theta)$$: the prior (we choose it)

But what about the **denominator** $$p(D)$$?

$$p(D) = \int p(D \mid \theta) \cdot p(\theta) \, d\theta$$

This is an **integral over the whole parameter space**!

**Concrete example:** Suppose you have a regression:
- 3 parameters $$\theta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma)$$
- $$n = 100$$ data points

Then $$p(D)$$ is a **103-dimensional** integral:

$$p(D) = \iiint \prod_{i=1}^{100} \mathcal{N}(y_i \mid \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i, \sigma^2) \cdot \mathcal{N}(\beta_0) \cdot \mathcal{N}(\beta_1) \cdot \text{HalfNormal}(\sigma) \, d\beta_0 \, d\beta_1 \, d\sigma.$$

**No one can compute this by hand!**

### 1.3. The surprising solution: "Don't compute, measure!"

Instead of trying to compute the integral, **we measure by sampling**.

This is the idea of **Monte Carlo**:

If you have a scale with a randomly swinging needle (completely random, no known formula!), and you weigh $$S$$ times and get the numbers $$\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \ldots, \theta^{(S)}$$, then:

- **Sample mean**: $$\bar{\theta} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \theta^{(s)}$$ will be close to $$\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D]$$ when $$S$$ is large
- **Sample percentiles** will be close to the credible intervals

This is the **Law of Large Numbers** - one of the most important theorems in probability and statistics:

$$\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} g(\theta^{(s)}) \xrightarrow{p} \mathbb{E}[g(\theta) \mid D] \quad \text{as } S \to \infty.$$

> **Summary of step 1:** We do not compute the integral. We draw samples from the posterior and then take the average!

### 1.4. But... where do we sample from?

Now we run into the **biggest paradox**:
- To compute the integral, we need to draw samples from the posterior
- But the posterior is exactly what we are trying to find!
- We do not know the formula of $$p(\theta \mid D)$$ in order to sample from it directly

**Solution: Build a "road" to the posterior**

Instead of sampling directly, we build a **chain of points** $$\theta^{(0)}, \theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \ldots$$ such that:
- We start from somewhere
- Each step "probes" a little
- After many steps, the points $$\theta^{(t)}$$ land in exactly the posterior distribution!

This is the idea of **Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)**.

## 2. Building the "road" to the posterior

### 2.1. How does a Markov chain work?

**Picture this:** You are lost in a forest (the parameter space) and want to find the highest mountain peak (the region of highest probability).

**The Markov chain way:**
- From your current position, you **look around** a little
- **Move to a new position** with higher probability (if you see an uphill path)
- **Stay put** if the new position is not better (with some probability)
- Repeat thousands of times!

The key point: **You do not need to know where the "peak" is** - you only need to know "which direction is uphill" (comparing heights between two points).

**Mathematical definition:** A Markov chain is a sequence $$\theta^{(0)}, \theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \ldots$$ in which the next position depends only on the current position:

$$p(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^{(t-1)}, \theta^{(t-2)}, \ldots) = p(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^{(t-1)}).$$

### 2.2. The "destination" of a Markov chain

An important property of Markov chains: **If run long enough, it will "forget" the starting position and converge to a limiting distribution** $$\pi(\theta)$$, called the **stationary distribution**.

$$\pi(\theta') = \int p(\theta' \mid \theta) \pi(\theta) \, d\theta.$$

**Ergodic theorem:** If a Markov chain is:
1. **Irreducible** - can reach every region from every other region
2. **Aperiodic** - has no fixed cycle
3. **Positive recurrent** - the mean return time is finite

...then a unique stationary distribution $$\pi(\theta)$$ exists, and every initial distribution will converge to $$\pi(\theta)$$.

> **The secret of MCMC:** We construct the Markov chain so that the **stationary distribution is exactly the posterior $$p(\theta \mid D)$$**! Then, after enough steps, the samples $$\theta^{(t)}$$ are exactly samples from the posterior!

## 3. The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm: "Building a road to the peak"

### 3.1. The idea: Try a step, reject if it is not good

You are at position $$\theta$$. How do you decide where to go?

**Step 1 - Proposal:** From $$\theta$$, you "close your eyes" and propose a new position $$\theta^*$$:
- **Random walk**: $$\theta^* = \theta + \epsilon$$, with $$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$$ (a small random step)
- **Independence**: $$\theta^* \sim q(\theta^*)$$ (a proposal independent of the current position)

**Step 2 - Compute the acceptance ratio:**
Compare the "height" at the two points:
$$\alpha = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta^* \mid D) \cdot q(\theta \mid \theta^*)}{p(\theta \mid D) \cdot q(\theta^* \mid \theta)}\right).$$

> **The wonderful point:** This formula **does not need to compute the denominator** $$p(D)$$! Because:
$$\frac{p(\theta^* \mid D)}{p(\theta \mid D)} = \frac{p(D \mid \theta^*) \cdot p(\theta^*)}{p(D \mid \theta) \cdot p(\theta)}$$
...we only need the ratio of likelihood × prior!

**Step 3 - Decide:**
- Flip a random coin $$u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$$
- If $$u \leq \alpha$$: Accept! Move to $$\theta^*$$
- If $$u > \alpha$$: Stay! $$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t$$

### 3.2. Illustration in code

```python
def metropolis_hastings(p_log_pdf, theta_0, n_samples, proposal_sd):
    """
    p_log_pdf: log posterior (unnormalized) - only needs log p(D|θ) + log p(θ)
    """
    samples = [theta_0]
    theta = theta_0
    
    for i in range(n_samples):
        # Step 1: Propose a new position (random walk)
        theta_star = theta + np.random.normal(0, proposal_sd)
        
        # Step 2: Compute the acceptance ratio (log scale for safety)
        log_alpha = p_log_pdf(theta_star) - p_log_pdf(theta)
        
        # Step 3: Decide
        if np.log(np.random.uniform()) < log_alpha:
            theta = theta_star  # Accept and move on
            
        samples.append(theta)
        
    return samples
```

### 3.3. Why does this algorithm work? (Mathematical proof)

**Question:** How do we know this chain will converge to the correct posterior?

**Answer:** Because we design it so that **Detailed Balance** is satisfied:

$$p(\theta \mid D) \cdot p(\theta' \mid \theta) = p(\theta' \mid D) \cdot p(\theta \mid \theta').$$

That is, the probability of going from $$\theta$$ to $$\theta'$$ (times the probability of being at $$\theta$$) equals the probability of going from $$\theta'$$ to $$\theta$$ (times the probability of being at $$\theta'$$).

**Check:**
- Transition probability: $$p(\theta' \mid \theta) = q(\theta' \mid \theta) \cdot \alpha(\theta \to \theta')$$

Consider two cases:

**Case 1:** $$\theta' \neq \theta$$ (there is movement)

$$\alpha(\theta \to \theta') = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta' \mid D) \cdot q(\theta \mid \theta')}{p(\theta \mid D) \cdot q(\theta' \mid \theta)}\right).$$

Let $$r = \frac{p(\theta' \mid D) \cdot q(\theta \mid \theta')}{p(\theta \mid D) \cdot q(\theta' \mid \theta)}$$.

- If $$r \geq 1$$: $$\alpha = 1$$, always accept moving toward higher probability
- If $$r < 1$$: $$\alpha = r$$, accept with probability $$r$$

We can prove that the balance equality is satisfied.

**Case 2:** $$\theta' = \theta$$ (staying in place)

The probability of staying includes: (rejecting a move to another position).

Because **Detailed Balance is satisfied**, $$p(\theta \mid D)$$ is the stationary distribution. By the ergodic theorem, the chain will converge to $$p(\theta \mid D)$$!

### 3.4. Tuning: How to choose the step size?

With a symmetric Gaussian proposal, the formula simplifies to:

$$\alpha = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta^* \mid D)}{p(\theta \mid D)}\right).$$

**Golden rule:**
- A step that is **too small** → almost every step is accepted but the chain barely moves → acceptance rate ~90% (but moves very slowly)
- A step that is **too large** → often rejected because it "jumps" over low-probability regions → acceptance rate ~5%
- **Sweet spot**: Acceptance rate **23-50%** depending on dimension

## 4. Gibbs Sampling: "Divide and conquer"

### 4.1. The problem with Metropolis-Hastings

When there are **many parameters** (for example 100 parameters in a neural network), random walk Metropolis-Hastings runs into trouble:
- It has to move in a **100-dimensional** space all at once
- The probability of "aiming" in the highest direction is $$\approx 0.5^{100}$$ → almost 0!
- The acceptance rate is extremely low, and the chain moves very slowly

### 4.2. The idea: One small step at a time

**Gibbs sampling** solves this by: **"Don't try to jump in all 100 dimensions, jump one dimension at a time!"**

With a model $$\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_K)$$:

**Initialization:** $$\theta^{(0)} = (\theta_1^{(0)}, \ldots, \theta_K^{(0)})$$.

**For each step $$t$$:**
1. $$\theta_1^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t)}, \theta_3^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_K^{(t)}, D)$$ - Keep the other parameters fixed, only update $$\theta_1$$
2. $$\theta_2^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t+1)}, \theta_3^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_K^{(t)}, D)$$ - Now that $$\theta_1$$ has been updated, update $$\theta_2$$
3. Continue until $$\theta_K$$

**Concrete example:** Suppose the model is $$\theta = (\alpha, \beta)$$.

**Step 1 - Sample $$\alpha$$ conditionally:**
$$p(\alpha \mid \beta, D) \propto p(D \mid \alpha, \beta) \cdot p(\alpha).$$

We only need 1 dimension - much easier to compute!

**Step 2 - Sample $$\beta$$ conditionally:**
$$p(\beta \mid \alpha, D) \propto p(D \mid \alpha, \beta) \cdot p(\beta).$$

### 4.3. Gibbs = Metropolis-Hastings with accept = 100%

<div class="textbook-theorem" markdown="1">
**Theorem:** Gibbs sampling is a special case of Metropolis-Hastings with acceptance probability = 1!
</div>

Indeed:
- Proposal: Change one parameter $$\theta_j$$ using a value from the conditional distribution
- The ratio works out to 1 (no division needed!)

**Advantage:** Always accepts → moves more efficiently!

**Disadvantage:** You must know the formula of the conditional distribution for each parameter (not always available!).

## 5. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: "Skiing downhill"

### 5.1. The next limitation

Gibbs is good, but it needs to know the conditional distributions. What if we do not know them?

**HMC** comes to solve this:

### 5.2. The idea: Use the "gradient" to slide

**Picture this:**
- You are at the top of a mountain and want to get down to the foot
- Instead of a random walk, you **ski**!
- The gradient tells you which direction is "downhill"
- Momentum helps you go farther and not get stuck in local minima

**Mathematics:**
1. Add "momentum" $$r$$ for each $$\theta$$
2. Hamiltonian: $$H(\theta, r) = -\log p(\theta \mid D) - \log p(r \mid \theta) = U(\theta) + K(r)$$
   - $$U(\theta)$$ = potential energy = $$-\log p(\theta \mid D)$$ (height)
   - $$K(r)$$ = kinetic energy = $$\frac{1}{2}r^T M^{-1} r$$ (velocity)
3. Simulate the Hamiltonian system using **leapfrog integration**
4. Accept with ratio $$e^{-H(\theta^*, r^*) + H(\theta, r)}$$

### 5.3. Advantages

- **Moves far**: Leapfrog creates large jumps, not a small random walk
- **High acceptance rate**: Thanks to using the gradient to "push" toward high-probability regions
- **Higher efficiency**: Especially in high-dimensional spaces

**NUTS (No-U-Turn Sampler)** automatically chooses a suitable number of leapfrog steps by running until the chain "turns back" (U-turn). This is the default sampler in PyMC!

## 6. How do we know the chain has converged? (R-hat and ESS)

### 6.1. R-hat: Do the different "roads" arrive at the same place?

**Problem:** How do we know the chain has converged?

**Solution:** Run **multiple independent chains** from different starting positions. If they all converge to the same distribution, the chains "merge".

**Formula:**
- **Within-chain variance**: $$W = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{1}{S-1} \sum_{s=1}^{S} (\theta_{m,s} - \bar{\theta}_m)^2$$
- **Between-chain variance**: $$B = \frac{S}{M-1} \sum_{m=1}^{M} (\bar{\theta}_m - \bar{\theta}_{..})^2$$
- **Estimated variance**: $$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{S-1}{S}W + \frac{1}{S}B$$
- **R-hat**: $$\hat{R} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{W}}$$

**Interpretation:**
- $$\hat{R} \approx 1$$: The chains have converged, between-chain variance ≈ within-chain variance!
- $$\hat{R} > 1.1$$: **Warning!** The chains have not converged - they are in different places!

### 6.2. ESS: Are the samples "independent"?

**Problem:** MCMC samples are **autocorrelated**. A chain of 2000 samples is **not equivalent** to 2000 i.i.d. samples.

**Picture this:** If you walk in small steps, the positions you step on will be **close together** → not independent samples!

**ESS measures:** The number of i.i.d. samples equivalent to the current MCMC chain.

$$\text{ESS} = \frac{S}{1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho(k)}$$

where $$\rho(k)$$ is the autocorrelation at lag $$k$$.

**Rule of thumb:**
- ESS > 400: Enough for estimating the mean
- ESS > 1000: Enough for quantile estimation  
- ESS < 100: **Warning!** Samples are too correlated

## 7. When to use which algorithm?

| Algorithm | Advantages | Disadvantages | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Metropolis-Hastings** | Simple, flexible | Slow random walk, low acceptance rate | Complex distributions, no alternative |
| **Gibbs Sampling** | 100% acceptance, simple | Needs known conditional distributions | When there are conjugate structures |
| **Hamiltonian MC** | High efficiency, moves far | Needs gradient, more complex | High-dimensional spaces (default in PyMC) |
| **NUTS** | Automatic tuning | Tuning overhead | PyMC/PyMC3 (default sampler) |

## 8. What this lesson wants you to keep

> **3 key ideas:**
> - **Metropolis-Hastings** works because it ensures detailed balance with the posterior as the stationary distribution
> - **Gibbs sampling** is MH with an acceptance rate of 100% when the conditionals are known
> - **HMC/NUTS** is a highly efficient version thanks to using the gradient and momentum

MCMC is not "magic" - it is a rigorous mathematical consequence of the theorems on Markov chains and Monte Carlo. When you understand it deeply, you will:
- Be confident in diagnostics because you know what R-hat and ESS measure
- Choose a sampler suited to the problem
- Debug when a sampler runs into problems
